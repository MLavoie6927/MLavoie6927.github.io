Painting: the way I work

These are my practical painting notes for smooth walls, rougher walls, cut-ins, and exterior work. I use a simple paint grade scale to think about cost and longevity.

WallsCut-insExteriorPaint gradesJob checks

Interior walls

Smooth walls

For a smooth wall, I use a 1/8-inch smooth-nap roller for both the primer and the regular paint. I want a clean finish without heavy roller texture.

Rougher walls

For a rougher wall, I start with a 3/8-inch nap for primer so it reaches more texture. Then I prefer a 1/8-inch nap for the regular paint to keep the finish as smooth as possible.

My primer color practice: I tint the primer lighter than the final paint color because I have seen it dry darker. I check a dried sample before committing to the full wall.

Brush loading and cut-ins

Load only the tip

I dip the brush about one inch into the paint. This keeps the brush controlled, with less paint on it and fewer drips. I tap off excess before cutting in.

My brush choice

For crisp cut-ins, my favorite is the Sherwin-Williams blue-tipped brush. I use it for the edge along ceilings, corners, and trim, then roll the nearby wall while the edge is workable.

Exterior painting

My heat cutoff

I avoid painting the outside of a house when it is above about 80–85°F, especially on sun-exposed siding. I would rather wait for cooler conditions than have the coating dry too fast while I work.

Application choices

One option I use is to roll the exterior primer and then spray a fine finish. For the most durable job in my experience, I prefer to roll and brush the house, working paint into the surface rather than relying on spray alone.

I aim for a long-lasting exterior job, potentially around 20 years with the right preparation, materials, conditions, and upkeep. That is my goal, not a promised service life for every home.

My paint grade scale

This 1–5 scale is my field shorthand for comparing price and the longevity I expect from a well-prepared job. It is not an industry grading system, product warranty, or guarantee. Prices are approximate per gallon and can change by store, region, and date.

Checks before starting a real job

  • Identify the surface, repair defects, clean it, and let it dry. Choose a primer compatible with that surface and topcoat.
  • Test my nap and primer-tint choices on a sample. Confirm the chosen coating reaches the texture and covers as expected.
  • Check the product label for application temperature, surface temperature, humidity, film thickness, drying, and recoat time.
  • For older homes, check lead-safe requirements before disturbing paint. EPA guidance for paid work in pre-1978 housing.
Why I verify the product instructions

Manufacturer guidance can differ from my preferred method: common drywall often uses a 3/8-inch cover, very rough surfaces can need a longer nap, primer shades vary with topcoat color, and exterior service life depends on climate and substrate. These checks help me adapt the notes to the specific job.
