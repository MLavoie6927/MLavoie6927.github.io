Intelligence / systems thinking / decision support

Know the systems.
Test the story.

My view is that an intelligence officer should understand the core of major jobs across sectors. Deep specialists are essential, but an analyst also needs enough breadth to see how logistics, food, energy, medicine, engineering, technology, finance, government, and human behavior affect one another.

03 / Analytic discipline

From a question to a decision

1

Frame the decision

Who needs to act, by when, and what would change their choice? Define scope, geography, and time window.

2

Map the system

Draw actors, inputs, dependencies, single points of failure, incentives, and normal baselines.

3

Compete explanations

List plausible alternatives and evidence that would disconfirm each one. Seek specialists where depth matters.

4

Evaluate sources

Track provenance, independence, timeliness, bias, collection gaps, and lawful handling. Two copies of one rumor are one source.

5

State the judgment

Separate observed facts from inference; explain confidence, uncertainty, alternatives, implications, and indicators that would change the call.

6

Revisit and brief

Give a concise recommendation, owner, and review trigger. Update the assessment when new evidence arrives.

This method is informed by ODNI analytic standards and the CIA's discussion of competing hypotheses.

04 / Collection begins with a decision

Name the customer, choice, and clock

A broad question such as “What is happening to food distribution?” is too open to guide collection. Turn it into a decision request and stop collecting when the answer is sufficient for that decision.

Decision request

Customer: regional procurement lead. Choice: whether to reserve alternate transport and shift perishables to a second distribution center. Deadline: 16:00 local time today. Scope: three affected counties and the next seven days.

Answer requirements

Estimate the likely duration and affected routes; identify what is observed, inferred, and unknown; compare the cost of acting now with the cost of waiting. Ask which confidence threshold the customer needs before changing the plan.

Stop rule

If another source is unlikely to change the decision before the deadline, issue a qualified brief now and set a review time. An unresolved question belongs in the collection gaps, not in an endless search.

05 / Depth through specialists

Know enough to ask better questions

Map the workflow yourself, then identify where specialist knowledge is essential. Record the specialist's role, firsthand access, assumptions, and what their answer can and cannot establish.

1

Logistics operator

“Which handoff is delayed—unloading, warehouse intake, or line haul? What timestamp or metric would distinguish them?”

2

Food safety specialist

“Which products lose usable life first, and what temperature or dwell-time records would verify risk?”

3

Retail or finance lead

“How many days of stock remain by product and location? Would an alternate route solve the problem at an acceptable cost?”

Do not treat one expert's explanation as an independent measurement of the whole system. Seek people positioned at different handoffs, and preserve disagreement.

06 / Source validation

Evaluate the specific item, not its label

The 30 source types above are a collection menu. A particular item earns weight only after its provenance, independence, time, access, and legal handling are checked. A public report repeating a distributor's claim is not a second independent confirmation.

Handling gate

Public availability does not remove privacy, copyright, safety, or terms-of-use obligations. Restricted systems, intercepted communications, personal records, and private telemetry require appropriate authority. This portfolio exercise uses synthetic observations and claims no such access.

07 / Fictional worked example

Why are regional food deliveries late?

A decision maker asks whether a three-day delivery slowdown is likely to persist. All details below are synthetic teaching examples, not live reporting.

Observations
  • A public port bulletin reports reduced berth availability.
  • A weather record shows heavy rain in the port area.
  • Two distributors report longer receiving times; both may rely on the same carrier.
  • Retail shelf photos suggest gaps, but capture dates and locations are unverified.
Competing explanations
  1. Port bottleneck: inbound containers are late.
  2. Inland transport: trucks or rail cannot move stock.
  3. Demand spike: normal delivery cannot keep pace.
  4. Reporting artifact: selected stores are unrepresentative.
Cross-sector tests

Compare port throughput with carrier ETAs, road closures, warehouse dwell time, agricultural shipment cycles, retail sales, and weather recovery. Ask a logistics operator which delay would appear first and a food safety specialist whether perishables face a different constraint.

Assessment & action

Provisional assessment: there is evidence of a localized logistics disruption, but its cause and duration remain uncertain. Do not infer a regional food shortage from shelf photos alone.

Next: confirm route-level delays and inventory cover; brief procurement to review alternate routes. Reassess if independent throughput and delivery data diverge from baseline.

Recruiter-facing deliverable

This page demonstrates a repeatable framework: sector map → source plan → alternative hypotheses → qualified judgment → decision and review trigger. It is an educational portfolio artifact, not a claim of intelligence officer employment or classified collection.

08 / Adversarial checks

Try to break the preferred explanation

Ordinary error, selective reporting, coordinated influence, and deliberate deception can all make a pattern look stronger than it is. Do not infer an influence operation from disagreement alone; assess provenance and behavior before assigning intent.

Counterintelligence mindset

Ask who had access to each fact, whether a source benefits from a particular conclusion, whether accounts are independent, and whether a narrative is amplified beyond the underlying evidence. Protect sensitive source details and avoid attributing an actor or motive without supporting evidence.

09 / Decision product

A one-page intelligence brief

This is the finished product for the fictional exercise. It distinguishes observations from judgment and gives the customer a decision they can use before the deadline.

Synthetic training exampleRegional food distribution · Prepared for procurement lead · Decision due 16:00 local

Localized delivery delays: cause and duration still uncertain

Key judgment
A local transport disruption is plausible, but available observations do not establish whether the primary bottleneck is at the port or inland, nor whether it will last seven days. Confidence: low, because independent shipment and inventory records have not been reviewed.
What we know
A public port notice reports reduced berth availability; a weather observation records heavy rain; two distributors report late receiving. The distributor reports may share one carrier. Shelf photos have unverified date and location.
What we assess
Some deliveries may be affected. A regional food shortage, weather as the sole cause, deliberate interference, and duration beyond the observed window remain unproven.
Alternatives and gaps
Inland transport constraints, a demand surge, and unrepresentative reporting remain credible. We lack route-level shipment timestamps, warehouse dwell time, and representative days of stock.
Decision support
Ask logistics to price and reserve a reversible alternate route for critical perishables; confirm whether inventory cover justifies activation. This is an option for the procurement lead, not a claim that diversion is already necessary.
Collection and review
Before 16:00, obtain independent port-to-warehouse timestamps and representative inventory cover through authorized channels. Review again at 09:00 tomorrow, or sooner if verified deliveries or stock fall below the customer's threshold.
Feedback loop

At the next review, compare predicted route delays and stock effects with actual records. Log what changed, which sources were reliable, which assumptions failed, and whether the decision threshold was appropriate. Revise the assessment rather than defending the first version.

Build the skill deliberately

Choose one question each week. Interview or read a specialist, map the sector's workflow, collect two independent source types, write competing explanations, and produce a one-page assessment with confidence and a review trigger. Over time, the maps become a cross-sector reference you can actually use.
