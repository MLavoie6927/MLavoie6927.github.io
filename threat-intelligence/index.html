Operation Harbor Lantern

A plausible account compromise becomes a mail collection problem. The analyst must decide what the evidence proves, what remains uncertain, and which actions are justified now.

02 / What is assessed

Competing explanations

H1 · Unauthorized use

Someone with access to the account created the rule to collect invoice-related mail. The unfamiliar sign-in, timing, and targeted external forwarding support this explanation.

Working assessment: most consistent with the supplied evidence.

H2 · Authorized automation

A user or approved workflow created the rule during legitimate vendor processing. This would explain the rule but needs owner confirmation and a documented business reason.

Test: confirm with the mailbox owner and change records.

H3 · Administrative activity

An administrator or delegated service acted on the mailbox. The provided audit set does not show a clear admin change, but collection is incomplete.

Test: review delegated access and admin audit logs.

Analytic judgment: Moderate confidence in unauthorized mailbox use, low confidence in how access was obtained, and no basis for naming a threat actor. No claim of successful exfiltration is made without message-trace evidence.

ATT&CK context

The scenario is consistent with T1078 Valid Accounts and T1114.003 Email Forwarding Rule. T1566 Phishing is a possible access path to investigate, not an observed fact here. ATT&CK labels describe behaviors; they do not identify the operator.

Evidence that would raise confidence

Owner denial together with corroborated session and rule attribution would strengthen the unauthorized-use assessment. Confirmed external delivery would establish data movement, subject to destination and content review.

Evidence that could reverse it

An approved change, owner confirmation, and matching device or delegated-access evidence could favor authorized automation or administrative activity. Update the judgment when the record changes.

05 / What to do

Decisions tied to evidence

Now · Contain and preserve

  • Disable the suspicious rule and preserve its configuration and audit trail.
  • Revoke active sessions and reset credentials using the organization’s incident process.
  • Check MFA methods, recovery options, delegated access, and other mailbox rules.

Next 24 hours · Scope

  • Run message trace for messages that matched the rule and verify delivery status.
  • Search for related sign-ins and rule changes across accounts.
  • Ask the mailbox owner about legitimate automation and recent sign-in activity.

Next 30 days · Reduce recurrence

  • Alert on new external forwarding rules and unusual sign-in plus mail-setting sequences.
  • Review external forwarding policy and exceptions.
  • Exercise a response playbook and test that logs contain the needed fields.

Likely business impact: invoice and supplier correspondence could expose payment details, negotiations, and contact information if matching mail was delivered externally. The exercise does not establish any financial loss.

06 / Branch on findings

Decision paths, not a single script

If no delivery is found

Retain containment and investigate attempted collection. Verify message-trace coverage and retention before concluding no exposure. Document the gap if coverage is incomplete.

If external delivery is confirmed

Preserve message IDs, delivery evidence, recipient, and affected content scope. Escalate through incident, privacy, legal, and business processes as applicable. Do not infer financial loss from delivery alone.

If activity is authorized

Record the approving owner and change evidence, review whether the forwarding destination and rule meet policy, then adjust the incident disposition and any control exception.

Executive update example

“We identified a new external forwarding rule following an unfamiliar sign-in to a procurement mailbox. The rule and sessions are disabled. We are checking whether any messages were delivered and whether the activity was authorized. At this point, exposure and access method are unconfirmed. The next update will report message-trace and owner-verification results.”

Detection handoff

  • Alert on creation or modification of external forwarding rules, retaining account, actor, destination, rule name, and timestamp.
  • Correlate a new rule with recent unusual sign-ins or MFA changes; tune against approved service accounts and known business workflows.
  • Test with an authorized synthetic rule and known benign change. Confirm the audit and message-trace fields exist before claiming coverage.

What remains unproven: credential theft, phishing, actual exfiltration, broader compromise, and threat-actor identity. The exercise shows an analytic method, not a production incident or live detection result.

08 / Engineering handoff

Turn the hypothesis into a testable analytic

Signal and joins

  1. Start with a mailbox rule create or modify event that has an external forwarding destination.
  2. Join on account and a recent identity sign-in; use session ID only if both sources actually expose a reliable common value.
  3. Raise priority for a new client, unusual sign-in, or recent MFA change. Preserve the raw records for review.
  4. Correlate message trace separately to determine whether a matching message was sent or delivered.

False positives and blind spots

  • Approved finance workflows and delegated administrators can create legitimate external rules.
  • IP geolocation, new-browser labels, and failed attempts are context, not proof of compromise.
  • Some forwarding paths or hidden rules may not appear in a simple mailbox-rule view.
  • Missing message trace prevents a confident no-delivery conclusion.

Portable logic sketch

RULE = mailbox_audit where action in (create_rule, update_rule)
       and forwarding_destination is external
SIGNIN = identity_log where result = success
CANDIDATE = RULE join SIGNIN on account
            where 0 <= RULE.time - SIGNIN.time <= 30 minutes
PRIORITY = new_client or unusual_signin or recent_mfa_change
OUTPUT = account, rule_id, destination, sign_in_id,
         event_times_utc, source_event_ids, raw_record_links
EXPOSURE = separate message-trace review, never inferred here

Implementation boundary: This is vendor-neutral pseudocode, not a deployable detection rule. Validate field names, event coverage, join keys, time normalization, approved exceptions, and test data in the actual platform before enabling an alert.

Analyst acceptance criteria

  • A test rule and sign-in produce one correlated candidate with source event IDs.
  • An approved workflow is visible and can be dispositioned with owner and change evidence.
  • A missing sign-in or message-trace source is reported as a coverage gap, not a clean bill of health.
  • The alert text distinguishes rule created, suspected unauthorized use, and confirmed external delivery.

09 / Finished product

Two-minute intelligence briefing

Key judgment · Moderate confidence: The synthetic procurement account appears to have been used to create an external forwarding rule after an unfamiliar sign-in. The rule was disabled and sessions revoked at 09:06. Authorization, access method, and external message delivery remain unresolved in the starting packet.

Why it matters: If matching invoice mail was delivered, supplier details and payment correspondence may have been exposed. At present the impact is a potential confidentiality risk, not a verified loss.

Decision requested: Maintain containment while the response team obtains owner confirmation, mail-rule history, and message trace. Escalate privacy and business review when evidence establishes the affected messages and applicable obligations. Report a revised assessment after those checks.

What would change this briefing?

Owner denial plus corroborated session detail raises confidence in misuse. An approved change and matching device or delegated-access record can reverse it. Delivery evidence changes the impact statement from possible exposure to a scoped, confirmed transfer; content review is still needed.
